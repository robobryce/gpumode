ABSTRACT
The emergence of neural engines such as Nvidia TensorCore GPU brings a revolution to deep
neural networks, as the neural engines can perform extremely fast general matrix multiplications.
However, how to deploy other algorithms or applications on neural engines remains questionable.
In this dissertation, I explore the possibilities of using TensorCore GPU to accelerate BLAS3
operations, linear algebra algorithms, machine learning algorithms on GPUs, hybrid CPU-GPU
architecture, and distributed systems.
Specifically, I design TensorCore-based matrix computation algorithms that can work on different architectures. My work on single GPU includes implementing some of the basic linear algebra
operations, which can be used in further matrix factorization. In terms of matrix factorization,
I develop the recursive QR factorization that utilizes the TensorCore GPU efficiently. I also try
to use TensorCore to accelerate the 2-stage Eigen Value Decomposition. In addition, I also tried
to migrate the scalable CPU-based support vector machine tool to TensorCore, which exhibits
a significant speedup and shows better performance than the state-of-art GPU-based SVM software. On the CPU-GPU hybrid architecture, I go a step further by investigating the recursive
strategy, then do a case study of out-of-core QR factorization using the recursive strategy, and
the results prove that the recursive algorithm works much better than the conventional algorithm.
On the distributed memory system, my current work is developing a unique data structure named
Universal Distributed Array (UDA), which has excellent programming flexibility and can utilize
TensorCore as well. Generally speaking, the TensorCore-based algorithms typically have a very
high performance, but it has to face the accuracy loss problem because of using half-precision.

Introduction
Driven by the need to train large-scale neural networks and exascale computing, many neural

engines have been introduced in recent years. These neural engines use half-precision and aim to
significantly accelerate the general matrix matrix multiplications (GEMMs) based on half-precision
utilization. The most often used neural engines include Google’s Tensor Processing Unit (TPU)1 ,
Intel’s latest FPGA, CPU, and Nervana Neural Processors, and Nvidia TensorCore GPU on its
Volta, Turing and Ampere architecture. In this dissertation, I’ll focus on discussing the Nvidia
TensorCore GPU.
As a matter of fact, the neural engines can only perform GEMMs. Although by the support
of lower precision arithmetic (such as 16-bit floating-point format), the GEMMs on neural engines
such as TensorCore on V100 can boast up to 120 TeraFlops (120 × 1012 floating point operation
per second) [105], the other applications and algorithms hardly ever benefit from such colossal
acceleration.
Several factors prevent algorithms from obtaining acceleration from the neural engines, as we
all know that the half-precision, no matter the FP16 or the bfloat16 standard, is not as accurate
as of the floating-point format (FP32). However, many numerical linear algebra algorithms are
susceptible to the accuracy loss from low precision. Thus, these algorithms are hard to use in
neural engines directly, even though some of them are rich in GEMMs. Another factor is more
prominent; for instance, the neural engines are firstly designed to hasten the training process of the
deep neural networks because most of them have many GEMMs. Therefore, the algorithms, which
do not have many GEMMs, are typically not able to benefit from neural engines. In addition, my
previous work indicates that some algorithms with unique shapes and sizes of GEMMs cannot be
performed quickly on neural engines either [121]. As a result, deploying ordinary algorithms on
neural engines needs more investigation.
1

https://cloud.google.com/tpu/

However, matrix computations on neural engines, including some numerical linear algebra algorithms and machine learning algorithms, are still attractive because the neural engines can bring
up to 15x speedup (Nvidia A100 GPU). However, how to make these algorithms work on neural
engines is challenging. Two problems must be solved: 1) how to deal with the accuracy loss caused
by the half-precision; 2) how to ensure the algorithms have enough GEMMs with suitable shapes
and sizes. In the following sections of this dissertation, I will address the design and strategy of
the matrix computation algorithms and what I do to tackle the above two challenges.
Obviously, the matrix computation algorithms are still the first choice to utilize neural engines
efficiently, even though they have some difficulties. Matrix factorizations are very close to this
research scope, and they are very likely to be used in many other matrix computations. Kernelbased machine learning algorithms are also very relevant because they naturally have large GEMMs
and are not sensitive to accuracy.
Matrix factorization usually decomposes a matrix into a product of several matrices, and typically these matrices are pretty special. For example, LU factorization decomposes a matrix into a
product of an upper triangular matrix and a lower triangular matrix, and QR factorization decomposes a matrix into a product of an orthogonal matrix and an upper triangular matrix. Usually,
the matrix factorizations are the intermediate step of solving some mathematical problems, e.g.,
LU factorization in solving linear systems and QR factorization in solving linear least square problems. These matrix factorization algorithms have large GEMMs (but the shapes and sizes might
be imperfect), and some iterative methods can typically refine the accuracy loss. To sum up, the
matrix factorization algorithms are crucial, and they have the conditions to be performed on neural
engines.
Not only the deep neural networks but also the large-scale machine learning algorithms are very
likely to have large GEMMs with pretty good sizes and shapes. The kernel-based machine learning
algorithms, which map the data to a higher or infinite dimension, have very large GEMMs, where
neural engines come in.

2.1

Half Precision Arithmetic and TensorCore Technology

Half precision, also referred to as binary16, is defined in the IEEE 754 standard. Unlike singleprecision (also called FP32), it only occupies 16 bits rather than 32 bits. It is intended for storage
and computing efficiency at the cost of losing accuracy. The early usage of half-precision is in
Nvidia’s Cg Language [78] in early 2002; and in late 2002, Nvidia implemented the half-precision
in its GeForce FX GPU. The half-precision is only used in some graphic algorithms at the very
early stage until Nvidia released the TensorCore Technology in 2017 on its Tesla architecture [105].
Resources about Nvidia TensorCore include detailed micro-architecture analysis and benchmarking [68], an early report on the programmability, performance, and precision [80]. Other than halfprecision GEMMs, researchers show that TensorCore can also be used for reduction and scan [23].
In [54, 57, 56, 75], TensorCore was used for accelerating linear system solvers in the framework
of hybrid CPU/GPU linear algebra package MAGMA[32]; and TensorCore based QR factorization [121] mentioned before. There are numerous use cases of half-precision or even lower precision
in the application of neural networks.
The roundoff error analysis of half-precision floating-point arithmetic is only emerging. The
report [62] provides some statistical roundoff error analysis that is more suitable for half-precision,
as the traditional deterministic analysis is too pessimistic to give any useful error bound. The
report [16] provides error analysis for the fused multiply-addition matrix multiplication on TensorCore. These papers [18, 19] proposes and analyzes a mixed half, single, and double-precision linear
solver.

2.2.1

Numerical Linear Algebra Algorithms
BLAS3 Operations

BLAS means Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms, which prescribes a set of low-level linear algebra
routines. It has three levels. Level 1 BLAS (BLAS1) is vector vector computations, including inner
products, vector norms and so on. Level 2 BLAS (BLAS2) is vector matrix computations, such
as general matrix vector multiplications. And level 3 BLAS (BLAS3) is obviously matrix matrix
computations like GEMMs, SYRK, and TRSM. There are several related implementations of BLAS.
LAPACK [4], which has been introduced before, is the most famous. ATLAS [115] provides an
auto-tuning version of BLAS routines, and the Intel Math Kernel Library (MKL) optimizes the
BLAS routines on Intel CPUs. On GPUs, the cublas libary2 is the most widely used library, and it
is also our benchmark in this paper; and MAGMA [32] provides several CPU-GPU hybrid BLAS
routines. Research has also proposed some valuable works on optimizing BLAS routines. Goto has
dedicated a lot to this field, including analysis of the performance of GEMMs on CPU [49] and
optimal BLAS3 operations [50]. These routines have been integrated into the gotoBLAS [48].
The recursive BLAS3 algorithms are not that popular and not widely referenced, and they are
often affiliated with the recursive linear algebra algorithms. Lawra [1] proposes recursive Cholesky
and LU factorization, attached with recursive TRSM and SYRK. ReLAPACK [89] also provides
recursive Cholesky and LU factorization, but it focuses on some other high-level BLAS3 operations,
such as solving the inverse of a matrix. Recursive QR factorization has been discovered by [40] and
was migrated to TensorCore [121]. Later in 2020, our work [122] shows that the recursive formula
of BLAS3 operations on TensorCore has better performance.
2.2.2

QR Factorization

The QR factorization, along with LU and Cholesky factorization form the half of important matrix
factorizations in numerical linear algebra. QR factorization can be used to solve linear systems,
LLS problems, orthogonalization of a set of vectors and eigendecompositions; see the encyclopedic

stable, one called LSQR [88].

2.2.3

Eigen Value Decomposition

The investigation of the symmetric eigenvalue decomposition problem has a very long history. And
the algorithms and methods in this area have been extensively studied.
Tridiagonalization and Tridiagonal Solve: The most widely used and well-known algorithm
is the QR algorithm [113], which repeatedly calls the QR factorization and GEMM and finally
converges to a diagonal matrix that contains eigenvalues on its diagonal. However, before the
QR and GEMM iterations, a tridiagonalization step is usually processed as a ’preconditioner’ to
reduce the number of QR iterations. Typically, the tridiagonalization is performed by Householder
transformation [47] and to improve the efficiency of the execution on modern high-performance
architectures, the WY representation technique [9, 96] is generally applied to the transformation
process. Another tridiagonalization method is called 2-stage tridiagonalization [58], which firstly
reduces the matrix to a band form (1st stage) and then reduces the band form to a tridiagonal
matrix (2nd stage). This method is proved to be very efficient on multi-core architectures [55, 76].
Another popular method is divide and conquer, and this method is implemented in most of
the linear algebra packages, including LAPACK [2], MAGMA [108] and CuSOLVER. Another
flexible method is called bisection [25], which aims to find a subset of eigenvalues, such as the
largest/smallest 100 or all eigenvalues within interval [a, b]. Furthermore, in 2004, the MRRR [30]
method was proposed. It seeks to compute accurately orthogonal eigenvectors without expensive
(O(n3 ) worst-case) reorthogonalization.
Other Eigen Decomposition Approaches: Polar decomposition [60] decomposes a matrix
into a product of an orthonormal matrix and a positive definite symmetric matrix. It is connected
to EVD and SVD, so recently, some new algorithms based on polar decomposition have been
proposed. QDWH-eig (QR-based dynamically weighted Halley Eigenvalue decomposition) [85] uses
QR factorization to compute the polar decomposition and then factorize the derived orthonormal
matrix using the iterative subspace method. Later in 2016, a GPU implementation [104] of QDWHeig and QDWH-SVD was proposed, but this work replaced the QDWH-eig with the 2-stage EVD.
9

There is also another method to compute polar decomposition called scaled Newton [17] that has
lesser mathematical operations than QDWH. However, it highly relies on the backward stable
inverse of a matrix.
Recently there has been growing interest in randomized Linear algebra [81], particularly randomized subspace iteration for computing a low-rank approximate eigenvalue/singular value decomposition. Two algorithms among them are randomized subspace iteration [52] and randomized
block Lanczos [119]. These two algorithms are proven to be very efficient in real-world applications, especially on modern high-performance architectures [97]. However, these algorithms are
typically based on multiplying a randomly generated matrix which means they can only be applied
to applications that are not sensitive to accuracy.
2.2.4

Out-of-core Linear Algebra Algorithms

The out-of-core processing has a long history, but there is an approximate chronological boundary
that before the year 2000, the out-of-core processing was usually deployed on a single disk-CPU
hybrid system, while after the year 2000, it was often used on CPU-GPU hybrid systems. In the late
2000s, the development of GPGPU and distributed systems attracted many researchers to explore
how to use GPU to deal with a massive scale of data, leading to the usage of out-of-core processing
on CPU-GPU hybrid systems and distributed systems. Since 2017 various devices such as NVIDIA
TensorCore, Google Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), and so on are quickly emerging in response
to the high demand for neural network training and inference tasks. The matrix multiplication
speed becomes so fast that the biggest problem fitting into the device memory would only take a
second to finish. However, without out-of-core capability, such impressive speed cannot be used to
solve a larger problem that does not fit into the very limited device memory space. Highly efficient
out-of-core matrix computations are highly desirable due to the accessibility and simplicity of a
single machine.
In 1996, SOLAR [106] was released, and it became the first well-implemented out-of-core package. It combines LAPACK [4] and ScaLAPACK [15] based in-core subroutines, out-of-core subroutines and input-output subroutines. Later in 2002, Toledo and Rabani [107] proposed an out-of-core
10

filter-diagonalization method to solve the very large electronic structure calculations. Note that
they also proposed an out-of-core recursive QR factorization, which is not included in the SOLAR
package in this work.
With the development of distributed systems, researchers are more interested in solving large
problems on distributed machines or multiple threads architecture rather than on a single machine. As a result, out-of-core processing is investigated in distributed systems. D’Azvedo and
Dongarra [26] migrated LU, QR, and Cholesky subroutines in ScaLAPACK to out-of-core relative
subroutines. And in 2012, Gregorio et al. [93] designed some out-of-core subroutines that do not
lose any performance on multithreaded architectures.
The cuBLASXt 5 library offered by Nvidia provides hybrid CPU-GPU implementations of
BLAS3 routines. In 2016, BLASX [112] used a different out-of-core strategy, and the performance
was slightly better than cuBLASXt. In addition, both cuBLASXt and BLASX aim at the multipleGPUs platform.
Some other CPU-GPU OOC applications solve specific problems. For example, both [69]
and [77] discuss the OOC SVD implementation on CPU-GPU hybrid system. Moreover, in our
previous work [124], the out-of-core recursive QR factorization strategy has been proved to be more
efficient on TensorCore, and the method is very likely to migrate to other linear algebra algorithms.

2.3

Kernel-based Machine learning Algorithms

PSVM [21] is a MPI based parallel implementation of low-rank Gram matrix approximation proposed in [43]. It is perhaps the closest to our work in that they both try to approximate the Gram
matrix using a low rank approximate to reduce computation burden, and also use the primal-dual
interior-point method for solving the approximated quadratic program. The most salient difference
is the low-rank approximation algorithm and the linear solver in the Newton steps. In PSVM, the
low-rank approximation is accomplished by a pivoted incomplete Cholesky factorization in factored
form. It boasts amazing time complexity O(nk 2 ) just by looking at all elements in the Gram matrix
5

https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cublas/index.html#using-the-cublasXt-api

11

once will incur O(n2 ) complexity and numerical robustness in the Newton linear solver because of
the incomplete Cholesky factorization. However, PSVM has two major disadvantages compared to
TensorSVM: 1) the low-rank approximation quality is significantly lower, thus usually requiring a
much larger rank k to obtain enough accuracy to get decent prediction accuracy; 2) the operation in
the incomplete Cholesky factorization with pivoting is essentially BLAS2 operation with low arithmetic intensity, which can only be executed at less than 1% of the rate as TensorCore on GPU.
We are not aware of GPU implementations of PSVM algorithm. It will be interesting to further
research the prospect of PSVM on GPU and how it compares with TensorSVM and ThunderSVM.
As large-scale kernel SVM is challenging to train, various approximation methods are developed
to make the training time manageable, usually at the cost of prediction accuracy. One approach that

3.1

Half Precision Arithmetic

Let us take a look at the different floating-point formats and see what gives and what takes in terms
of accuracy (resolution in representing real numbers), and range (smallest and largest representable
real number).
The IEEE single-precision floating-point format is accurate and widely ranged, for it has 32 bits
to spare. There are currently two widely implemented 16 bits floating-point formats. Among them,
IEEE FP16 has a significantly constrained range, but its resolution (the unit roundoff error—the
distance to the following representable number from 1) is about 10 times better than bfloat16.
Bfloat16, on the other hand, has the same range as IEEE FP32, but its resolution is pitiful (there
is no bfloat16 number between 1 and 1.0078). Thus bfloat16 is more robust (less prone to overflow
and underflow) but less stable/precise (large roundoff error), see Figure 1. And in this dissertation,
I use the FP16 format supported by NVIDIA TensorCore.
Error analysis of such low precision arithmetic is only emerging. In [63] has error analysis
that shows accumulating in higher precision helps greatly in preserving accuracy in matrix/vector
accumulation.

3.2

TensorCore GPU

TensorCore GPU NVIDIA introduced a specialized unit called TensorCore from their Volta architecture, which boasts up to 120 TFLOPS (120 × 1012 floating-point operations per second) for
half-precision (FP16) matrix-matrix multiplication. Compared to single-precision SGEMM (Single
precision GEneral Matrix-Matrix multiplication) and double-precision DGEMM, TensorCore is 7x
and 14x faster on V100 GPU and 15x and 30x faster on the latest A100 GPU. And this is a considerable upgrade in the performance at the cost of significantly lower precision and consequent loss
of accuracy and numerical stability.

Basic Linear Algebra Operations

4.1

Backgroud

The goal of Level-3 Basic Linear Algebra Subroutiones (BLAS3) [35] is to perform a matrix multiplication update and are of the form C = C + AB, where C, A and B are matrices. The BLAS3
routines that I focus on in this section are TRMM, TRSM, SYRK. TRMM stands for triangular matrix
matrix multiplication, A = αLB, where α represents a scalar, L represents a lower or upper triangular m × m matrix and B represents a m × n matrix. TRSM represents triangular solve with
multiple right hand side, A = αL−1 B, where α represents a scalar, L represents a lower or upper
triangular m × m matrix and B represents a m × n matrix. SYRK represents symmetric rank k
update, B = αAAT + βB, where α and β represents a scalar, B is m × m symmetric matrix and
A is a m × k matrix. The current state of the art projects for BLAS3 operations are MAGMA [32]
and cuBLAS.
Modern computer architecture requires high computation intensity to allow sufficient data reuse
rate to achieve better performance, and this is the reason why current high-performance computing
algorithms emphasize using the blocking technique to increase data locality. As a result, many
BLAS3 algorithms are proposed, and they serve as the functional and performance workhorse for
many matrix computations, such as LAPACK [4]. We argue in this chapter that on Tensor Units,
specifically on NVIDIA TensorCore GPU, recursive algorithms often provide a better performance,
performance portability across generations of the device, and numerical accuracy. Particularly,
we investigate the recursive flavor of BLAS-3 algorithms, which promises higher performance on
TensorCore.
As the wide availability of Tensor Units only occurred recently, TensorCore-based applications
are emerging but remain relatively limited. Particularly, no BLAS3 TensorCore-based operations
exist except the GEMMs from vendor optimized library cuBLAS and the open-source CUTLASS.
As most matrix computations and factorization involve BLAS3 operations such as TRMM, SYRK, and
TRSM routines, it’s useful to devise such a package includes fundamental BLAS3 routines.

18

The general idea of implementing BLAS3 operations on TensorCore is using the recursive strategy, which can expose more data locality. And the experimental results show that the recursive
strategy has better performance than the conventional blocking strategy. Particularly, for TRMM
routine, even without TensorCore, the recursive strategy has better performance than the cuBLAS
STRMM routine.

4.2.1

Recursive vs. Blocking: Two Approaches to Expose Data Locality in Linear
Algebra Operations.

Modern processors provide a much faster computation speed than the main memory. To bridge this
gap, processors rely on hierarchical memory systems and exploit the principle of the locality to keep
the functional units busy. The motive behind it is to often reuse data available in the fast memory
(caches, registers) so that the functional units are not starved of data from the main memory. In
dense linear algebra, textbook algorithms often boil down to matrix-vector (BLAS2) or vectorvector (BLAS1) operations for simplicity of exposition; such algorithms perform poorly on modern
processors because BLAS2/BLAS1 operations do not have sufficient arithmetic intensity to allow
for locality. The performance will be bounded by memory bandwidth. In response to this hardware
trend, a blocking technique was developed [35] to aggregate multiple BLAS2 operations into matrixmatrix operations (BLAS3). When properly implemented, BLAS3 operations can exhibit sufficient
locality to provide data fast enough for the functional units to work at nearly full speed. Gradually
many higher-level linear algebra operations such as matrix decomposition’s are mostly blocked and
have achieved excellent performance on modern CPUs [4] and GPUs [32].
The traditional blocking algorithms are based on iterations, and the performance is susceptible
to the blocksize. Usually, the algorithms work on a relatively small panel, and the panel size
is termed as blocksize. Once the panel is processed, the algorithms updates the trailing matrix

19

using general matrix matrix multiplications (GEMMs). The blocksize should be chosen carefully to
balance the time cost of the panel and the trailing matrix update. Elaborating more on it, the panel
often involves much more BLAS2 and even BLAS1 operations, which are much slower than BLAS3
operations. As a result, the blocksize cannot be very large. However, the blocksize cannot be very
small either. To justify this, consider one extreme situation in which the blocksize is one, then the
update procedure (GEMMs) will perform general matrix vector multiplications (GEMVs), which
is simply a BLAS2 operation. This kind of blocksize prevents the update procedure processing the
trailing matrix from running at full speed. Thus, the blocksize should be selected carefully to avoid
imbalance between the panel and update stage, and this is what ALTAS [115] does.
However, with the widening gap between compute speed and memory speed, blocking becomes
inadequate to achieve near peak speed. For example, NVIDIA V100 GPU features specialized
functional units called TensorCore that perform matrix-matrix multiplication at an astonishing
speed. When it comes to GEMMs, the CUDA cores can use the GEMMs to reach peak performance
with small blocksize such as 512. But the new TensorCore typically requires the blocksize to be
larger than 2000 and, in some scenarios, even 4000 to run at full speed. Hence the blocksize should
be fairly large to exploit the TensorCore fully. As we mentioned above, a larger blocksize leads to
a slower panel. Meanwhile, the performance gap between BLAS2 and BLAS3 operations is even
more in newer architecture. A larger panel involving many BLAS1/BLAS2 operations inevitably
hurts the overall performance. So, there exists a contradiction: fast BLAS3 computations desire a
larger blocksize while slow BLAS2 computations prefer a small panel. This conflict is even more
significant in modern GPU architectures such as Nvidia Volta and Ampere.
An alternative way is to use recursion instead of iterations. This means we decompose a problem
into multiple subproblems, and finally, in the end, we merge the solutions of the subproblems to
obtain the solution of the initial problem. There are several advantages of recursive algorithms. It
is conspicuous that the recursive algorithms, compared to the iterative algorithms, are more likely
to provide larger GEMMs. Besides, based on the analysis of the recursive QR factorization [121],
the performance of the recursive algorithms seems to be insensitive to the cut-off size (in our

20

implementation of the recursive algorithms, the cut-off size represents the blocksize and the leaf
represents the panel term used above). As a result, we can probably ignore the influence of the
cut-off size by using recursive BLAS3 algorithms.
To be more specific, we provide an example to show the difference between blocking algorithms
and recursive algorithms for the triangular solve routine TRSM; refer Algo 1 and Algo 4. TRSM(A,B)
computes a matrix X such that AX = B, where A is a triangular matrix. This is a useful operation as many matrix decompositions generate triangular matrices and rely on triangular solve. The
textbook algorithms for triangular solve would be backward/forward substitution which processes
one column per iteration; however, such algorithms do not make use of the principle of locality. Algorithm 1 employs blocking, which processes NB columns per iteration. As a result, BLAS1/BLAS2
operations are confined to the small NB columns, and the rest are cast into matrix-matrix multiplication of size NB. In contrast, Algorithm 4 divides the matrix A into four quarters and X, B into two
halves and recurses into the half-size subproblems before combining the result with matrix-matrix
multiplication. The panel/leaf operations are exactly the same, and the difference is the shape and
size of the GEMMs. In fact, blocking TRSM generates a matrix multiplication of constant size
NB, whereas recursive TRSM generates matrix multiplications of size NB, 2NB, . . . , n/2. In general,
the GEMMs’ shape in recursive algorithms tends to be bigger and thus can be performed faster on
TensorCore. We will demonstrate the performance in more detail in the experiments section. See
Table 1 for how the TensorCore favors larger matrix.

QR Factorization
QR factorization is one of the most vital matrix factorizations in numerical linear algebra.

Its derived applications include Linear Least Square (LLS) Solver and Low-Rank Approximation
(LRA), and these applications play important roles in real-world problems. QR factorization aims
to factorize a matrix to a product of an orthogonal matrix Q and an upper triangular matrix
R, and it’s almost always an important building block of any numerical linear algebra packages
such as LAPACK [4], ScaLAPACK [15]. On GPU, Nvidia provides a well-optimized linear algebra
library named cuSOLVER, including LU, QR, and SVD decomposition. MAGMA [32] is a more
comprehensive tool that works on hybrid CPU/GPU architecture. The most performant and stable
algorithm (used in all the packages mentioned above across different architectures: distributed
memory, multi-core, GPUs, and single-core CPU) is blocked (tiled) Householder QR factorization.
We will typically use SGEQRF to denote such implementation in single-precision from cuSOLVER.

5.1

Background

QR factorization decomposes a matrix A into a product of an orthogonal matrix Q and an upper
triangular matrix R. There’re typically three methods to factorize a matrix, including GramSchmidt, Householder transformation, and Givens rotation, and these three methods have their
own advantages and disadvantages. For the classic and modified Gram-Schmidt QR factorization,
they need fewer flops to generate Q and R at the cost of lacking stability. The Householder QR
factorization needs less flops to form R only, and it’s very stable to handle with ill-conditional
matrices. But when the explicit Q is required, the algorithm will spend 2x flops on generating Q.
For Givens rotation, it usually serves for sparse matrices, which is not in this dissertation’s scope.
So, in the rest of this subsection, we’ll only give Gram-Schmidt and Householder QR factorization
background knowledge and skip the QR factorization based on Givens rotation.

Linear Least Square (LLS) Problems

LLS problems are prevalent in natural science, engineering, and data science. To give a specific
example (gradiometry), consider the large-scale least square problems solved today concerning
the determination of the Earth’s gravity field from highly accurate satellite measurements [39].
Another example is the least square problems arising from many areas (data fitting, statistical
machine learning, geodesy, computer vision, robotics (bundle adjustment), etc.). Non-linear least
square problems can often be solved as linear least square problems.
The (over-determined) linear least square problem is stated as an minimization problem:

min ||Ax − b||2

(14)

where A ∈ Rm×n (m > n) has full column rank, and b ∈ Rn . Geometrically, this minimization is to
find the ”projection” of point b onto the range (column space) of matrix A. Analytically the LLS
problem has closed form solution:
x∗ = (AT A)−1 AT b

(15)

Computationally, the analytical solution can be obtained by solving the square linear equation
(called the normal equation): AT Ax = AT b. Typically a Cholesky factorization of AT A = LLT can
lead to a solution via backward and forward substitution; this method is called normal equations.
However, directly forming AT A is unstable for all but the most well-conditioned systems; in practice,
we would avoid forming AT A directly.
A much more reliable and accurate method is based on QR factorization. A tall and skinny
matrix A takes roughly 2x flops than the normal equations method, but it handles a much wider
range of matrices. The basic idea is as follows. First, we factorize the rectangular matrix A ∈
Rm×n , m > n into the product of an orthogonal matrix Q ∈ Rm×n , and a square upper triangular
matrix R ∈ Rn×n : A = QR. Then the solution to (14) is given by the following elementary

36

matrix-vector operation:
x∗ = R\(QT b)

(16)

5.2.1

Gram-Schmidt QR Factorization on TensorCore
Performance Analysis

To analyze the performance of QR factorization on TensorCore, we cannot get around a question:
why can’t we just replace all matrix multiplication with TensorCore and get higher performance?
Actually, this is what MAGMA does in the TensorCore-based LU factorization [56].
Unlike matrix-matrix multiplication, matrix factorization typically exhibits more dependency
and less parallelism and a more complicated memory access pattern. Therefore, matrix factorization
cannot achieve the speed of matrix-matrix computation, but with algorithmic innovations called
”blocked” or ”tiled” QR could approach a significant fraction of peak performance. The idea of
the tiling is to aggregate matrix-vector operations into fewer but bigger matrix-matrix multiplications to increase arithmetic intensity (ratio between operations and number of elements) therefore
enabling better data locality in the trailing matrix update. This technique is essential in bridging the gap between fast processor and slow memory, using the fast on-chip memories (registers,
caches) to service most memory access. But because of the complex dependency, some part of the
factorization is still matrix-vector and vector-vector based, which have much lower data locality.
Modern algorithms and implementations usually divide each iteration of the factorization into two
steps: panel factorization (slow, but small) and trailing matrix update (fast and big matrix multiplication), where most the floating-point arithmetic is spent in the trailing matrix update. Based
on this structure, a straightforward strategy is to leave panel factorization intact while replacing
the trailing matrix update with TensorCore GEMM. However, this simple strategy is not enough
to yield significant speedup for QR for the following reasons:
The matrix multiplication is not completely on the critical path. The MAGMA [33]
QR uses a hybrid CPU/GPU architecture where panel factorization is on CPU, and trailing matrix

39

update (GEMM) is on GPU. Due to the algorithmic pipeline, the GEMM execution is completely
or partially overlapped by the panel; thus, speeding up GEMM has only a limited effect on the
overall QR speed. See Table 4 for an illustration. It seems to suggest that just replacing the trailing
matrix update with TC-GEMM leads to underwhelming speedups.

5.2.3

Communication Avoiding Gram-Schmidt QR Factorization Panel

The recursive Gram-Schmidt QR seems to be able to benefit from TensorCore substantially, but
the unaccelerated panel factorization (cuSOLVER SGEQRF()) limits the overall speedup severely.
In this subsection, we once again trade computations for more data locality (fewer data movements/communication) in the panel, by employing the communication avoiding Gram-Schmidt
QR.

44

The challenge in fast panelQR is data locality and parallelism. The conventional Householder
panel has sequentially dependent iterations, and the working-set is the whole panel that cannot fit in
fast memory on GPU (register files+ shared memory). Fortunately for QR, there’s a communication
avoiding QR (CAQR) [3] variant that simultaneously improves parallelism and data locality at the
cost of more computations. Our panelQR is based on CAQR, with the Modified Gram Schmidt QR
replacing Householder QR used in [3]. The idea of CAQR can be illustrated in equation (13).
Practically, we fix our panel to be of 32 columns with m rows and decompose the matrix A into
256x32 submatrices (step ¬). On V100 GPU, the 256x32 submatrix can fit into shared memory so
that we only need to read and write global memory once. These 256x32 blocks are independently
factorized using the modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm into QR factors; see Algorithm 7. To map
this algorithm to GPU effectively, we let each threadblock QR factorizes one 256x32 block. We
launch 256 threads, with each thread reading, processing, and writing a single row of the 256x32
block. The most time-consuming part is line 7, where reductions are needed (vector inner products
across threads). We use the CUB template library8 from NVIDIA Research to have a threadblock
level fast reduction. We manually unroll the loop 4 ways to expose more instruction-level parallelism
and to reduce the number of reductions by a factor of 4. In step ¯ we use cuBLAS batched SGEMM()
subroutine to do the matrix multiplications in parallel. We recurse in step ® until the number of
rows is below 256 so that a single threadblock will suffice.
In summary, our CAQR implementation has two salient features: 1) the Gram-Schmidt process
is run completely within shared memory; 2) all the inter-threadblock communication/synchronization
happens in the batched SGEMM(), which is extremely fast. Hence our CAQR panel reads global
memory minimally (log8 (m/256) passes to the panel) , and have minimal cross threadblock synchronization and communication.
For a 32768x128 panel, our hand-coded CAQR panel achieves 0.33TFLOPS, which is 3.3x
faster than cuSOLVER SGEQRF(). As a result, the RGSQRF() algorithm equipped with the new

Orthogonalization:
Another critical application of QR factorization is called
orthogonalization— finding an orthogonal basis of the columns of matrix A (which is given by Q
in the QR factorization). The RGSQRF algorithm inherits one of the deficiencies of the GramSchmidt orthogonalization procedure in the loss of orthogonality of the Q factor for ill-conditioned
matrices. If we need accurate orthogonalization results for some applications, we need to refine the
orthogonality of the Q factor. To do that, we can re-orthogonalize the Q factor again using the
RGSQRF subroutine: Q = Q2 ∗ R2 . This procedure is akin to the block re-orthogonalization in
Gram-Schmidt QR [103]. The general result can be summarized by “twice is enough”: orthogonalizing twice should bring the orthogonality to fully working precision. For some analysis behind this
technique, we refer to [44]; here, we show some empirical study that demonstrates the practical
efficacy of re-orthogonalization. From Figure 18 we see that the backward error (||A−QR||/||A||) is
not affected by cond(A) for both RGSQRF and SGEQRF, and it’s up to the full working precision,
half and single, respectively. From Figure 19 we see that SGEQRF is fully orthogonal up to working
precision, whereas RGSQRF’s orthogonality deteriorates with the increase of cond(A). However,
with re-orthogonalization, RGSQRF-ReOrtho can be brought back to orthogonality up to working
precision. With re-orthogonalization, RGSQRF is 3.7x to 7.7x faster than SGEQRF+SORMQR
from Figure 20.

5.3.2

Implementation and Optimization

The implementation consists of panel implementation and GEMMs implementation. We will discuss
them separately.
As we mentioned in the previous section, our Householder panel performs better than RGSQRF’s
modified Gram-Schmidt panel, even if our householder panel has to do more arithmetic operations
(4mn2 − 34 n3 vs. 2mn2 ). It’s mainly because the performance of the panel is memory-bound rather
than computing-bound. Despite doing much more arithmetic, our well-optimized CUDA kernel
panel performs even better than RGSQRF’s panel.
Particularly, we fix our panel to be 32 columns with m rows and divide the panel into 256 × 32
sub-matrices. On Titan V GPU, the 256×32 sub-matrices can be fit into shared memory; therefore,
we just need to read and write global memory once. These sub-matrices are factorized independently
using the Householder QR algorithm into QR factors. To map this algorithm to GPU effectively,
we let each threadblock factorize one 256 × 32 block. For each block, we launch 1024 threads,
and we have 32 wraps. Each wrap is in charge of one single column: computing norm, scale, and
addition. The rest of the wraps update their columns if necessary (e.g., computing (I − vv T )x).
After the Householder vectors are generated, we further form the explicit Q. The CUDA kernel
follows similar tasks decomposition and mapping strategies. A critical operation in the kernel is the
reduction operation required by the inner product of the columns. Since each warp is in charge of
each column, the reduction is among the threads within each warp such that shared memory is not
required for the reduction. We implement warp-level all-reduce based on __shfl_xor_sync(). Our
62

CAQR panel is around 2.5x faster than SGEQRF despite performing more than 2x the arithmetic.
At the very beginning of the implementation of reconstructing Householder vectors, we thought
that we might need our own kernel for LU factorization and triangular solver because we’re not clear
if the cuSOVLER and cuBLAS provide well-optimized routines for non-pivoting LU factorization of
small matrices and triangular solvers for tall and skinny matrices. Fortunately, with the experimental result from Table 7, we found LU and STRSM only account for less than 10%. Hence, we don’t
put effort into optimizing LU factorization and STRSM; instead, we call cuSOLVER SGETRF() and
cuBLAS STRSM() routines directly. Note that if the matrix is extremely tall and skinny, optimizing
LU factorization and STRSM for such tiny matrices may lead to even more tremendous speedups.
On the GEMMs aspect, based on the experimental results and our performance model, in terms
of GEMMs, when the matrix size (number of columns or number of rows) is small (less than 128),
TC-GEMM performs worse than GEMMs. Thus, we disable TensorCore when the matrix size is
small.

6.1

Background

In this section, we will introduce some background knowledge and some related technologies and
algorithms to band reduction.
6.1.1

Tridiagonalization

The tridiagonalization process is usually the pre-step of eigenvalue decomposition. The goal of
tridiagonalization can be expressed as follows:

A = Q−1 × T × Q
Q is an orthogonal matrix, and T is a tridiagonal matrix. The conventional method of using
Householder reflections eliminates the elements except for the tridiagonal part. However, only 50%
of the computations can be fully blocked (BLAS3). In other words, 50% computations are BLAS2
operations that cannot benefit from modern hardware. Indeed, indicated by the MAGMA ssyevd
routine experimental results, the unblock computations take over 90% of the execution time of
the tridiagonalization (ssytrd routine). An alternative way to increase the blocking percentage
is called 2-stage tridiagonalization [58]. This method adds the successive band reduction before
the tridiagonalization. Mathematically, the successive band reduction can be expressed by this
equation: A = Q−1 × B × Q where B is a band matrix with bandwidth b. Moreover, this is the
first stage. The second stage reduces the band form to a tridiagonal form, and it has called bulge
chasing [11] and the performance of the 2nd stage depends on the bandwidth b. Figure 25 shows
the general steps of the 2-stage tridiagonalization.
The tridiagonalization is typically factorized based on Householder transformation, which has
already been introduced in Chapter 5.3.

Full to Band Algorithm

The symmetric matrix Householder update, also called 2-side update, is quite simple. See Figure 26.
The first block includes A1 and the grey region named Panel. Because we want the matrix to
be reduced to band form, only the Panel needs to be factorized. After the first step, we have
QR(P anel) = (I − W Y T )R, and the R matrix will override the upper triangular part of the Panel,
and meanwhile, the lower triangular part of the Panel will be set to be 0s. And the further trailing
matrix update is 2-side:
A2 = (I − W Y T )−1 A2 (I − W Y T )

Refined TCGEMMs

The Refined TCGEMM [87] is a TensorCore-based GEMM that provides the same accuracy as
CUBLAS SGEMM using FP32 but exceeds the theoretical peak performance of FP32, yielding a
much higher throughput. They are an enhanced version of the error correction methods proposed by
Markidis [79] to perform matrix-matrix multiplication on Tensor Core. Just the implementation of
error-correcting methods would not provide the accurate results of matrix-matrix multiplication on
FP32 SIMT cores. The key to achieving this high accuracy lies in the way: dealing with rounding
off inside Tensor Cores. Refined TCGEMM saves the mantissa loss and uses it to correct the
accuracy of matrix-matrix multiplication. This high-accuracy, high-performance, and low-power
consuming implementation is developed in NVIDIA’s CUTLASS and achieves 51TFLOPS/s for
limited exponent range and 33TFLOPS/s for full exponent range of FP32 using TF32 Tensor
Cores on NVIDIA A100 GPU. This implementation outperforms the theoretical FP32 SIMT Core
peak performance of 19.5TFLOPS/s.
To be more specific, consider a GEMM C = A × B. To compute this GEMM on TensorCore,
the truncation of A and B from FP32 to FP16 should be performed in advance. Then we have
A = Ã + ∆A and B = B̃ + ∆B, where the Ã and B̃ is stored in FP16 precision. The TCGEMM
with FP32 accumulation is actually doing C = Ã × B̃ and the accurate result should be C =
(Ã+∆A)×(B̃ +∆B) = Ã× B̃ + Ã×∆B +∆A× B̃ +∆A×∆B. In practice, the ∆A×∆B is so trivial
that it can be typically overlooked. Therefore, performing two extra TCGEMMs seems to recover
the accuracy loss. But the rounding errors inside TensorCore and the underflow problems still
prevent the naive recovering solution from getting accurate results. As a result, two other methods
are applied to solve the problem: 1) try to avoid the rounding for accumulation inside TensorCores
to recover the FP32 accuracy; and 2) try to scale the matrix to reduce underflow. And finally, the
refined TCGEMMs can outperform the SGEMMs and meanwhile bring the accuracy back to single
precision. And we’ll also show the performance impact of using the refined TCGEMMs in our band
reduction in Figure 33. Note that the refined TCGEMM method hasn’t been fully implemented;

Out-of-Core Linear Algebra Algorithms

7.1

Background

Out-of-core (OOC) means out of memory (when main memory was called core memory a long
time ago). However, OOC has received less attention than distributed parallel computing, but we
think OOC has its use cases in the era of data-centric computing. Both can enable solving bigger
problems that cannot fit in the memory of a single node, but the latter also entails using multiple
processing units. In the era of vector and matrix accelerators, we think OOC is becoming more
important as it enables an increasingly common use case because of the convenience of using a
single node and the ubiquitous accessibility of such a platform. Furthermore, for some problems,
a single accelerator can rival the speed of a medium-sized cluster of CPUs. For example, a single
A100 GPU with TensorCore has a peak performance at around 300 TFLOPS, which is equal to
thousands of modern high-end CPU cores with AVX512 vector units. Yet, the memory capacity of
the accelerator is often very limited. The limited problem size due to insufficient memory capacity
greatly limits the potential of the extremely fast accelerator.
A particular class of problems that potentially benefits from OOC with accelerators is dense
linear algebra. In this chapter, we explore techniques to enable efficient OOC computing on extremely fast accelerators such that the computing is no longer limited by scarce GPU memory.

memory capacity do not improve commensurately. Consequently, the algorithms used by state-ofthe-art works such as BLASX [112] are no longer adequate to hide all the communication costs.
For instance, in terms of the V100 PCIe graphic card, the peak rate of data movement is around

bottleneck.
Given the new situation, the TensorCore-based OOC application optimization needs to be data
movement centric. Without careful arrangement of data access patterns, the overall performance
will be dominated by the data movement rather than the computations. So, there are two main
challenges to designing high-performance OOC linear algebra algorithms. 1) How to reduce the
data movement between different memory levels; 2) How to overlap the data movement with computations.
To illustrate how to address these challenges, we select QR factorization as an example, as it
has a well-implemented in-core TensorCore-based algorithm [121]. And in the rest of this section,
we will try to explain why the conventional blocking algorithms cannot work efficiently and why
the recursive algorithms have better performance. Besides, we will also discuss how to implement
the OOC QR factorization with data movement perfectly overlapped.

7.2.1

Blocking Strategy

The blocking QR factorization’s workflow is shown in Figure 35. At first, we select a blocksize b
and factorize the first b columns (termed as panel). Then we have the factorized Q1 and let it time
the rest of the matrix A2 to get R12 . The next step is updating the rest of the matrix A2 . After
that, we start a new panel factorization and repeat the steps until the last panel is factorized. See
Figure 35 for the intuitive steps.
7.2.2

Recursive Strategy

Compared to the blocking strategy, the recursive strategy is not that popular. The reasons might
be that blocking algorithms give enough data locality and parallelism on previous architectures,
but now on the new matrix accelerators, it no longer can. The recursive algorithm is assembled

denoted by A = [A1 |A2 ]. At first, we factorize the first half A1 = Q1 R11 , and then compute
north-east quarter of R12 = QT1 A2 . Next we update the second half A2 = A2 − Q1 R12 . Finally,
QR factorize the updated second half A2 = Q2 R22 . Note that the QR of the two halves can be
recursed using this algorithm itself. Figure 36 shows the general steps of factorizing a matrix with

7.3

Implementation and Optimization

We’ve already shown the recursive QR factorization has better data movement behaviors and
overlapping ratio. The implementation and optimization are essential as well. So, in this section,
we’ll illustrate our implementation and optimization strategy. Note that we divide the optimization
into two parts. The first part is the optimization inside GEMMs, called GEMM-level optimization;
the second part is optimizing the data transferring between panel factorization and GEMMs, and
it’s termed QR-level optimization.
7.3.1

GEMM-Level Implementation and Optimization

Similar to the performance analysis, the two types of GEMMs have two different implementations.
Inner Product:
Typically, to implement a high concurrent CPU-GPU program, we should be clear about how to
use the streams. Cuda streams9 are introduced by Nvidia to solve the concurrency problems of
executing programs on the device. Generally speaking, with streams and proper devices (most
modern Nvidia GPUs support CUDA streams), moving data from device to host, from host to
device, and in-core computations can be executed at the same time. In our problem scenario,
moving data from host to device, in-core computations, and moving data from device to host can
work concurrently because computations can be overlapped by communications, and the host-device
communication links are bidirectional. Therefore, we need at least three streams to make these
three assignments run in parallel. But because it’s an inner product, it’s unnecessary to transfer
9

https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/gpu-pro-tip-cuda-7-streams-simplify-concurrency/

94

the matrix C from device to host before the entire computations are ended. Thus, we can only
let the move-in operations and the computations be asynchronous and move the data out in the
end. In this case, we create several streams, and each stream takes charge of one block. The final
pipeline is shown in Figure 42.
Outer Product:
In terms of this type of GEMM, as we discussed before, the B is already stored on the GPU (the
B is the result of the inner product, there’s no need to copy it from CPU to GPU again). So,
we only need to read tiles in A and C and write to the tiles in C. But because of the limited
GPU memory, we use the same GPU memory space to store the matrix C, which means the movein operation should be performed after finishing the entire move-out operation to avoid conflict.
This waiting impedes the performance because the move-out operation cannot be overlapped. To
solve this problem, we use extra memory space to store the C temporally, and once the move-out
operation is done, we perform a fast in-core data transfer. At the same time, we start another
move-in operation, see Figure 44.
Blocksize Matters:
The blocksize of GEMMs cannot be selected casually. Smaller blocksize leads to slower in-core
computation, and larger blocksize will increase the overhead of the first move-in and last move-out
operations. So we can follow this strategy: start with a relatively small blocksize and gradually
increase it to the max blocksize. Consider this situation, when we’re computing the largest inner
product in recursive QR with GEMM size 65536 ∗ 131072 ∗ 65536, and we set the blocksize to be
8192. Then the first step is to compute a GEMM with a size of 65536 ∗ 8192 ∗ 65536. Obviously,
if we start with blocksize 8192, the first move-in operation cannot be overlapped by computations.
But if we begin with blocksize 2048 and gradually increase the blocksize to 8192, then the same part
of the move-in operation of the first block (65536 ∗ 8192) can be overlapped. Actually, based on our
experimental results, this trick increases the inner product performance from around 85TFLOPs
to 87TFLOPs.

95

7.3.2

QR-Level Implementation and Optimization

So far, the optimization of OOC GEMMs has been done, but there are still some problems left. For
example, how to deal with small GEMMs? The m or n in small GEMMs is not large enough, so
the data movement cannot be overlapped perfectly. Indeed, we cannot optimize the small GEMMs
anymore because of the insufficient data intensity, but we can tackle this problem in the QR-level
optimization that can help us avoid unnecessary data movement. The optimization doesn’t happen
inside GEMMs but works between QR panel factorization and the GEMMs. Besides, using the
results from the inner product in the outer product to reduce unnecessary data movement can also
be regarded as QR-level optimization to some extent.
So, after answering what’s QR-level optimization and implementation, the question becomes
how to implement and optimize it. Actually, the implementation is quite easy; following the design
pattern of the in-core recursive QR [121] is the solution. But the difficulty is how to optimize it,
including cutting off the unnecessary data movement and enabling cross BLAS operation overlapping.
The first optimizing strategy is cutting off some move-in operations of the panel. Because when
the GEMM size is small, the entire results can be stored on GPU, and they can be used directly
in the next panel factorization. Similarly, when the GEMM size is small, it doesn’t really need to
read the matrix A from the CPU; it can directly use the panel factorization results and only read
B from the CPU.
The second optimizing strategy is trying to hide the move-out operations between panel factorization and GEMMs. For example, when the panel factorization is finished and the factorized
matrix is ready to be transferred to host, we can perform a move-in operation for the inner product
at the same time. This kind of overlapping can also happen between the inner product and the
outer product. When we’re moving R12 out, we can move in the first blocks of A and C. Also,
after the outer product, the last move-out operation can be overlapped by moving in the first few
columns of the panel. See Fig 47 for the intuitive optimization results.

Kernel Machine

8.1

Background

Support Vector Machines (SVMs) is a classic statistical machine learning models for classification
and regression. A survey conducted by Kaggle in 2017 shows that 26% of the data science practitioners use SVMs to solve their problems. For data-set with smaller feature space, SVMs can
employ kernel tricks to map low-dimensional data to high-dimension space to obtain a non-linear
model, which leads to a large and non-separable quadratic optimization problem with constraints.
It involves a kernel matrix of size n × n for a data-set with n training examples. However, at a large
scale, it’s infeasible to store this extremely large kernel matrix in memory and process it. There are
two different approaches to dealing with this challenge: one is to decompose the optimization problem into a sequence of smaller ones, and the other is to solve an approximate optimization problem.
The former approaches include the very successful and widely used LIBSVM package [65], and the
latter approaches include deterministic low rank approximation of the kernel matrix ([43, 21]), and
randomized approximation of the kernel matrix (Nystrom’s method [37] for example).
Computationally, the SVM is a representative statistical machine learning problem thus inherits
many of their challenges on a large scale. In this paper, we propose a novel randomized approximate
kernel SVM training technique called TensorSVM that is accurate, scalable to large scale data-sets
and parallel processing units, and efficient on GPU with TensorCore units.
Compared to the state-of-the-art approximate SVM solvers, TensorSVM is more accurate in
approximation which yields uncompromising prediction accuracy, and possesses better data locality that allows it to exploit hierarchical memory system and specialized TensorCore on GPU.
Compared to non-approximate kernel SVM solvers such as LIBSVM [65]/ThunderSVM [114], TensorSVM exposes much more parallelism, with lower asymptotic time complexity, and have predictable convergence rate and execution time.

8.1.2

Optimization Algorithms

As discussed in the previous subsection, the SVM training problem is formulated as a quadratic
program (23), with simple bounds inequality constraints (24) and a single linear equality constraint (25). There are many optimization algorithms that apply to this problem; however few of
them can handle millions of variables and constraints efficiently. The most successful and popular
one is a decomposition based coordinate descent like algorithm called sequential minimal optimization (SMO), and its subsequent improvements incorporated in software package LIBSVM [20, 65]
and ThunderSVM [114]. In its original scheme, SMO picks two variables and fix the rest, and
optimize the two-variable quadratic program using an analytical solution. Which variables to pick
is integral to the success of this algorithm, and heuristics are often used to guide the selection of the
two variables according to second order information. The resulting algorithm and software (LIBSVM/ThunderSVM) is elegant, robust, and fast. However, there are two undesirable properties of
this line of algorithms: 1) it’s hard to parallelize and scale, since SMO decomposes into a sequence
of extremely small problems; 2) the convergence speed vary greatly and is very sensitive to the data
matrix conditioning and the hyperparameters C, γ.
Another broad class of algorithms—second order, or Newton like algorithms—seem to be attractive at large scale, primarily due to its quadratic local convergence rate (correct digits double
in every iteration) and often superlinear global convergence rate, and its insensitivity to the conditioning of the quadratic program. Newton like algorithms often converge in a few dozen iterations,
but each iteration involves a linear solve with the Gram matrix, therefore entailing O(n3 ) operations. For n in the millions, O(n3 ) is ExaFlops which is extremely expensive. However, if the Gram
109

matrix is of low rank k  n, then the linear system can be solved in O(nk 2 ) time, which is the
motivation of approximating the Gram matrix with a low rank matrix (see the Subsection 8.2.1 for
details).
The specific second order methods that solve quadratic program with equality and inequality
constraints are called barrier methods or interior point methods. The basic idea is to turn the
inequality constraint into a soft-barrier with increasingly high penalty when it approaches violation
of the constraints. With the barrier, the program is approximated as a quadratic program without
constraints which can be solved using Newton’s method. A more effective, and easier to use the
interior point method is called primal-dual interior point method, which solves the perturbed KKT
conditions using Newton’s method. We will discuss the basics of the interior point methods we use
in more detail later.

8.2

Methods

The TensorSVM consists of two parts: the low rank approximation and the interior point method
to solve the approximate quadratic program.
8.2.1

Algorithms

here are two phases of the TensorSVM algorithm: phase I computes a low rank approximation for
the kernel matrix G using a method adapted from randomized rank-revealing QR factorization [83],
and phase II solves the quadratic program from the dual SVM optimization problem using the
approximated kernel matrix and primal-dual interior point method. We describe the two phases in
sequence.
Efficient and Accurate Low Rank Approximation of the Gram Matrix on GPU:
When training on large scale datasets the kernel matrix G ∈ Rn×n can be of tremendous size
and renders solving linear equation with it prohibitively expensive. Fortunately for a lot of kernel
functions and parameters, the kernel matrix can be very well approximated by a low rank matrix,
as it has rapidly decaying eigenvalues [101, 117]. Our low rank approximate is based on the random
projection method [83]. The idea is that we want to find an orthonormal matrix Q ∈ Rn×k with
the small number k columns such that the range of Q covers much of range of the matrix G we wish
to approximate: range(Q) ≈ range(G). It turns out that for a matrix Ω ∈ Rn×k with i.i.d random
Gaussian entries with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, the product range(GΩ) ≈ range(G) in the
expectation, and can be enhanced with extremely low variance with power refinement and slight
over-sampling. The trick is that GΩ has a smaller size than G so we can find an orthonormal
basis from this smaller matrix GΩ faster: Q = ortho(GΩ). In practice, a QR factorization can be
used to find an orthonormal basis of GΩ. See Algorithm 13 for our adapted algorithm which is

8.2.2

Algorithmic Complexity and Performance Model

The execution time of TensorSVM is quite predictable, in contrast to the highly unpredictable
ThunderSVM/LIBSVM. The reason is the observed superlinear convergence rate of interior point
method, which in practice never exceeds 100 iterations. In this subsection, we characterize the time
complexity and execution rate of TensorSVM.
The low rank approximation phase (Algorithm 13 and Figure 50) consists of the following
operations: 1) (step 1&4) generating the Gram matrix G ∈ Rn×n where Gij = yi yj exp(−γ||xi −
xj ||2 ) and xi ∈ Rd , i = 1, . . . , n are training samples; 2) (step 2,4,7) matrix multiplication; 3) (step
3) QR factorization; 4) (step 6) eigenvalue decomposition. Add them together we get the time

116

complexity of the low rank approximation:

T1 = O(n2 d) + O(n2 k) + O(nk 2 ) + O(k 3 )

The MPC phase (Algorithm 14) is iterative in nature but for all practical purposes the number
of iterations to convergence is constant (<100) due to its superlinear convergence rate. In each
iteration, step 1 and step 4 that solve a linear system is the dominant computations, which in turn
is dominated by the Sherman-Woodbury-Morrison formula in Algorithm 16. The time complexity
is, therefore:
T2 = O(nk 2 )
Adding this together we get the asymptotic time complexity:

T = O(n2 (d + k)) + O(nk 2 ) + O(k 3 )

Note that all the dominant computations are dense matrix-matrix multiplications or matrix factorization thus can be expected to be executed at a very high rate on GPU.
8.2.3

Streaming Computations

Since the matrices involved is completely beyond the capacity GPU memory and sometimes the
host CPU memory, the TensorSVM takes advantage of the pass-efficient algorithms and implement the TensorSVM with out-of-core processing, on-demand generation of the Kernel matrix, and
overlapping communication with computation.
Out of core implicit Kernel Matrix Multiplication To support training on millions of samples, the
lack of GPU device memory must be addressed because not only the Gram matrix (size n×n) cannot
fit into GPU memory, the low rank factor U alone may not fit. As such TensorSVM supports out of
GPU memory (out of core) processing by streaming the matrices to GPU in chunks. For example,
steps 1 and 4 in Algorithm 13 and Figure 50 invoke kernel matrix multiplication. We divide the
kernel matrix into 100,000 by 100,000 chunks and stream them to the GPU. The Gram matrix does
117

not even fit in CPU memory so we will re-generate the kernel matrix on-the-fly whenever it is in
the pipeline to be streamed to the GPU.
Out of core QR factorization.

Due to the limitation of GPU memory, processing whole QR

factorization for matrix A = QR is not possible if the size of the matrix A is too large. In this
paper, we use a QR algorithm named Communication Avoidance QR(CA-QR) [29] which allows
us to divide the matrix into smaller blocks and stream them to GPU for processing, and then
reassemble them to complete the QR factorization. See more details in Section 5.1.3.
8.2.4

TensorCore Acceleration

The emergence of TensorCore in NVIDIA GPUs and other tensor engines in accelerators or CPUs
provides tremendous potential to greatly accelerate numerical applications with high energy efficiency. However, outside of applications of neural networks, effective and robust use of TensorCores
is only beginning. Two challenges must be properly handled: 1) how to provide sufficient data locality to allow TensorCore to accelerate meaningfully? 2) how to mitigate the loss of accuracy
and stability due to the limited precision/range in the 16 bits floating point format? In this subsection, we consider carefully the use of TensorCore to accelerate TensorSVM. We show that the
low rank approximation (phase I) is conducive to TensorCore acceleration while the second order
optimization (phase II) is susceptible to numerical instability.
TensorCore is accurate and stable for low rank approximation (phase I, Algorithm 13) .

The

reasons are three folds. First, the Gram matrix G is well scaled by construction, and the right
factors (random Ω, ortho-normal Q) are well conditioned and scaled. This ensures that the matrix
multiplication is unlikely to have overflow which results in ∞ or completely incorrect result. Second,
the low rank approximation error is dominated by the truncation due to the low rank constraint.
Compared to a typical truncation error, the roundoff errors are insignificant and thus can be
ignored. Third, some potential instability is handled at low rank approximatino algorithm level as
discussed in Section 8.2.1. To give an example, let’s directly examine the low rank approximation
error of Algorithm 13 with and without TensorCore in Figure 51. From the figure, we can see the
error of TensorCore Randomized LRA closely tracks the error of single precision Randomized LRA
118

up to around relative error level 10−4 and then the TensorCore LRA error stops improving. This
is to be expected because the half precision arithmetic TensorCore uses has unit roundoff error
9.8 × 10−4 . It seems that 10−4 level of low rank approximation error is quite adequate for the SVM,

as supported by the observations in the experiment section, where enabling TensorCore in phase I
maintains prediction accuracy. To summarize, our particular low rank approximation is conducive
to TensorCore acceleration and resistant to instability caused by low precision and range of FP16.
TensorCore is unstable in Interior Point Method linear solver (phase II). Blindly applying
TensorCore to all matrix-matrix multiplication may quickly lead to instability. In fact, in the second
phase of TensorSVM, divergence results (Algorithm 16). The reason is that as the iteration in phase

119

II goes on, the linear system (I + Z T D−1 Z) becomes increasingly ill-conditioned (κ → ∞ at the
solution); moreover the SWM based algorithm exhibits excellent data locality but is not backward
stable. When D becomes ill-conditioned, computing Z T D−1 Z on TensorCore will deviate from the
solution (ZZ T +D)−1 b so much that the IPM iteration almost always diverge. To summarize, unless
the strongly backward stable algorithm is used, TensorCore will likely lead to cause instability. More
research is required to exploit TensorCore in phase II, which this paper does not explore.
8.2.5

Comparison to Other SVM Solvers and Low Rank Approximators

As TensorSVM consists of both innovations from algorithms and parallel computing, the performance advantage of TensorSVM in comparison with other approximate/non-approximate solvers
may come from contributions from both. However, the two contributing aspects (algorithmic and
technical) are naturally intertwined, because the algorithms are designed to better exploit the underlying architecture we are targeting (multiple GPU accelerators with neural engine units), and the
technical implementation takes advantage of the opportunities to accelerate and parallelize. Nevertheless, we attempt to compare TensorSVM to other non-approximate and approximate SVM
solvers with vastly different algorithms and implementation and comment on the performance aspect.
Low Rank Approximation.

Traditional deterministic low rank approximation such as Singu-

lar Value Decomposition and truncated rank-revealing QR factorization are inefficient in our case
because: 1) they are not pass-efficient, meaning that they require O(n) passes over the Gram
matrix which is very slow when the matrix does not fit GPU memory or even the CPU memory;
2) only half of the arithmetic is spent on matrix-matrix multiplication so that TensorCore can at
most accelerate them by 1.75x. A more suitable deterministic low rank approximation is based
on incomplete symmetric pivoted Cholesky factorization [43, 21]. This method features low complexity O(nk 2 ) and can be made pass-efficient, however, it suffers from low data locality and low
approximation accuracy (see Table 19 PSVM column). On the other hand, randomized low rank
approximation including Nystrom’s method [37, 120], and the Algorithm 13 are more promising in
that they are pass-efficient and arithmetic intensive to benefit from TensorCore. Nystrom’s method
120

randomly sample k columns of the Gram matrix to form C ∈ Rn×k , and approximate the Gram
matrix G with G ≈ CW + C T . Algorithm 13 randomly projects onto a subspace of range of G, in a
more similar fashion as randomized SVD or QR [82]. It seems that the Nystrom’s method approximation accuracy is inferior than Algorithm 13; see Table 19 LLSVM column. To summarize, the
proposed Algorithm 13 is accurate even with small rank, pass-efficient (only 2 passes), and can be
substantially accelerated by Tensor Units.
Structured Low Rank Linear Solver. In the interior point method with low rank matrix, each
iteration involves solving a dense structured low rank linear system D + ZZ T where D is diagonal
and Z ∈ Rn×k . There are two efficient algorithms that solve such system in O(nk 2 ) time rather
than O(n3 ): Sherman-Woodbury-Morrison (SWM) inversion formula in Algorithm 16, and factored
form Cholesky (FFC) factorization [43]. The former has excellent data locality but is sensitive to
ill-conditioning, whereas the latter has poor data locality but is stable. We chose the former as
structured low rank linear solver for its much better speed on GPU and avoid the instability by
terminating the optimization when the matrix D becomes too ill-conditioned, and fine tuning the
initial guess.
Numerical Optimization Algorithms: Second Order vs. First Order. To make use of the Tensor
Units the algorithm must be of very high arithmetic intensity, otherwise, the operations are memory bandwidth bound. Furthermore, the algorithm must be rich in matrix-matrix multiplication
like operations (the only operations supported by TensorCore). First-order optimization methods
(gradient descent, coordinate descent, etc) operate with the gradient which means vector-vector
operations hence low arithmetic intensity. Therefore LIBSVM/ThunderSVM does not benefit from
the presence of TensorCore. Furthermore, first order methods can converge really slow depending
on the condition of the optimization problem, which in turn depends on the input data and hyperparameters C, γ. On the other hand, the second order Interior Point Method used in TensorSVM is
observed to have superlinear convergence rate and converge in 100 iterations in all cases we tested.
For example, see Figures 52 and 53 for comparison on the same data set with different hyperparameters. From the figure we can see that TensorSVM execution time is insensitive to the different

9.1

Background

In the current day and age, data is considered to be the new oil, and to get a meaningful representation of the data from the processing context, the data set is stored in the form of a matrix
data structure which itself is a combination of different feature sets. Each feature set contains data
belonging to that particular feature set. The cardinality of the feature set is also referred to as the
dimensionality of the data set. And due to the advancement in technology and the prevalent age of
the internet has led to the exponential growth of data in context to the volume of data collected as
well as the velocity at which it gets generated. This leads to humongous data sets. And it gets arduous to process such a huge data set. Over time one important technique developed to reduce the
size of data set yet retain meaningful information is known as dimensionality reduction. Using this
technique, we only retain the dimensions that are statistically most relevant. And dimensionality
reduction comes in two flavors(a)no transformation is applied to the data in the feature set, we
just remove the less useful dimensions, and (b) dimensions are reduced by combining the feature
set and applying the transformation to the data in the feature set. Some of the advantages of
dimensionality reduction are (1) the data set takes less space, (2) reduces computation/processing
time of the data set, and (3) denoising of the data set. Other most common algorithms for dimensionality reduction are Principal component analysis (PCA) [99] for linear data and kernel PCA
for nonlinear data. Kernel PCA successfully computes principal components in a higher dimension for a nonlinear data set. One of the most used techniques for data compression is low-rank
approximations. Using low-rank approximation, we try to find an optimized version of the current
matrix such that the optimized matrix has less rank than the original matrix. One way to make
Low-Rank Approximation is via SVD [70].
In addition to the above techniques, to make computation and processing even more efficient
for big data, we need to turn to distributed architectures. The development of big efficient data
processing techniques along with the opportunities brings in the enhancement of current data

130

structures, computer architectures, and algorithms. Solving problems in the scope of big data has
several challenges that can be concluded to be hardware aspects and software aspects. Fortunately,
the researchers have already done a lot of valuable work regarding the storage, distribution, and
computation within the big data research area. For instance, the supercomputers and clusters,
which are integrated with high-performance CPUs and GPUs, are deployed in many institutes.
And the advancement in hardware leads to new theories, new algorithms as well as new tools
emerge. One of the most famous and widely-used tools is MPI [51] which is introduced in the
1990s. It builds a bridge between different computing nodes and allows them to communicate with
each other, thereby working together. Actually, MPI can be regarded as the cornerstone of other
algorithms that are based on multiple nodes. Based on the MPI programming norm, many parallel
algorithms and applications are published.

9.2

Methodology

In this section, we’re going to demonstrate our design pattern, code architecture, and corresponding
implementation. In more detail, we’ll discuss how we distribute the data on different processors,
how we construct our code architecture, and how we implement the Randomized Block Lanczos
Algorithm using our distributed array.
9.2.1

Data Distribution

In the ScaLAPACK and Elemental project, the data distribution is block-wise. In other words,
different blocks of the data are stored on different processors. In contrast, the data distribution of
a universal distributed array is element-wise, which means the data are grouped by elements rather
than blocks. Figure 57 gives an intuitive explanation of the distribution model. Suppose we have an
8x8 matrix, and we want to store the matrix on 4 processors. Definitely, the matrix can be divided
into 4 blocks (for example, four 4x4 blocks) and then stored the 4 blocks on 4 processors. Actually,
this strategy is used in ScaLAPACK and Elemental. But in our UDA, the data is arranged element
by element. As shown in Figure 57, we have a 2x2 grid, and processor P00 takes charge of the 1st,
3rd, 5th, and 7th elements in the 1st, 3rd, 5th, and 7th row; processor P01 is responsible for the

131

rest of elements at the same row as P00; so does P10 and P11. Compared to the block-wise data
structure, the load is more balanced because the granularity is one element instead of one block.

Figure 57: The data distribution of a 8x8 matrix on 4 processors with 2x2 grid in UDA

To support the unique data structure, we’ve defined two classes that can help us manage the
distributed array. One is LMatrix, and another is DMatrix. The LMatrix means local matrix, which
takes charge of the data stored on a single processor. Accordingly, the DMatrix means distributed
matrix, and it’s responsible for the communication between different processors such as broadcast
and gathering. Additionally, to allocate the resources, especially the MPI resources, we’ve defined
a Grid class to integrate and recombine the MPI functions.

9.3.1

The general steps of the randomized block Lanczos algorithm are shown in Algorithm 17 which

algorithm is very suitable for executing on a distributed system. It contains GEMMs, tall and skinny
QR factorization (TSQR) that have proven distributed implementations, reliable performance, and
an additional SVD operation performed locally. Secondly, this algorithm is beneficial and widely
used in many Big Data-related areas such as SVM [97, 123].

Implementation in Elemental

Elemental is also coded in C++, and the coding style resembles that used by its predecessor,
libflame. Elemental successfully hides most details about the matrix and vectors within objects to
avoid unnecessary clutter like in ScaLAPACK.

Similar to UDA, we first perform the GEMM operation. We redistribute matrix A to perform
AllGathers within rows and then redistribute matrix Omega such that it performs AllGathers
within columns. Like UDA, Elemental also considers a process mesh, p = r × c where r is the
number of row processes and c is the number of column processes used to achieve the block-cyclic
distribution. Each process performs a local GEMM and stores the result in another distributed
matrix K. Unlike UDA, Elemental does not support TensorCore for performing GEMM operations.
The GEMM operation is a blas::GEMM function implemented by the Elemental framework. The
redistribution of the matrix A and Omega and the GEMM is achieved by

DistMatrix<T, MC, STAR> A_MC_STAR = A;

Once we have the results of GEMM, we perform a tall and skinny QR factorization. Elemental
support the distribution of matrix in 2 formats. Processes are numbered either in row-major or
column-major. The cost of converting the matrix distribution from row-major to column-major is
minuscule and could be easily ignored. The TSQR in Elemental requires distribution of matrix A
by in column-major. Therefore, we redistribute the matrix A and perform TSQR as follows,

9.3.5

Flexibility and Productivity

While there are several excellent software libraries/frameworks available, distributed matrix and
tensor computations remain a niche area that are quite intimidating to non-experts. The lack of
developer friendliness is increasingly become a hindrance to the fast-evolving ecosystem in broader
areas that more and more rely on large scale data drive computations, such as machine learning.
Traditional distributed matrix libraries such as ScaLAPACK do not appear to have major adoption
in data-driven computation community, where the demand is high, but they often settle in suboptimal frameworks such as Dask [94] or attempt to come up with their own implementation
laboriously. To benefit broader computing community with decades of experience and efforts of
144

parallel computing primarily in the High-Performance Computing (HPC) community, more flexible,
modern, light-weight yet highly performant and scalable solutions are needed. Particularly, such
solutions should be easy to adopt, easy to extend, and easy to compose to adapt to new algorithms
that are being investigated and tried every day. In this section, we compare with the most modern
and user-friendly distributed matrix computation framework—Elemental—in terms of software
engineering and programmability.
Flexibility: The variables used in Elemental support several data types, including float, double,
DoubleDouble (QD’s dd real), QuadDouble (QD’s qd real), Quad (GCC’s float128), and BigFloat
(MPFR’s mpfr t and MPC’s mpc t). Typically, these data types are enough to handle ordinary
tasks. But in the past few years, driven by the need to train large-scale neural networks, other
data types such as half-precision(FP16, bfloat16) and short integer (INT8, INT4) are widely used.
Thus, building a project based on these new data types becomes impossible in Elemental unless
Elemental updates itself to support these data types. In contrast, the usage of data types in
the UDA framework doesn’t have such restrictions. The data type selection depends on which
packages or libraries to use in the project. The TensorCore-based GEMMs in Figure 61 are an
excellent example that the FP16 is automatically converted when using the half-precision cuBLAS
library.
The UDA framework also allows users to configure any library with minimal modification to
existing objects and functionalities. It is forward and backward compatible with most third-party
libraries and quickly upgraded to later versions. Unlike the UDA framework, Elemental has integrated many existing libraries such as LAPACK, PLAPACK, and ScaLAPACK. However, using
the excluded third-party libraries in the Elemental project is not very easy. When we’re testing
the code written by Elemental, the GEMM is extremely slow when using a 16 × 16 grid. As a
result, we’ve tried to use the inside LAPACK library to perform the local GEMM, but Elemental
does not seem to offer a SGEMM routine in its integrated LAPACK library. And it is also pretty
complicated to directly link to an external LAPACK library. Besides, suppose the external libraries
are updated. In that case, programming in UDA can take advantage of the updated libraries. In

145

contrast, programming in Elemental would have to wait for the methods to be compatible with the
latest version of the libraries.
Performance Optimization: We modify the code to amplify the benefits of performance,
throughput, efficiency, and optimal use of resources. But how do we determine if the code will
provide the best results without a comparative study? In UDA, we could write our functions
or leverage the accelerating performance and efficiency offered by the third-party libraries. We
can plug in and play with most third-party libraries with minimal modification to the existing
functionality. Our experiments use the LAPACK and cuBLAS libraries to provide an accelerating
performance. On the flip side, as we know, Elemental is less flexible. We have to re-write the
code using a third-party library each time to compare results. In this day and age, where time is
critical, and a lot of research is going on to develop performance-driven libraries, we must create
a framework that leverages these benefits with minimal effort and energy spent in re-writing the
code.
Development Productivity: Software engineering is difficult and costly, and performance
optimization, distributed computing all add more dimensions to the productivity problem. It seems
that data-drive computing is entering the explosion stage, which generates high demand for high
performance distributed computing software, but the fast-growing areas often are accompanied by
fast-changing algorithms and models. The foundational framework for matrix/tensor computations,
therefore, should be light-weight and highly productive otherwise, it cannot keep up with the pace
of new development.
To make sure the performance is good enough, a comparison is indispensable. When programming with Elemental, sometimes the users are not aware that the performance is not good. For
example, when we’re writing the tall and skinny QR factorization code, we call the inside TSQR
function (qr::ExplicitTS(Q, R)), but actually, the performance of this inside function is quite
low. However, with the comparison to the UDA implementation, we found that Elemental’s TSQR
function is relatively slow. Thus, to ensure better performance, a comparison with other packages

146

and libraries is necessary. Fortunately, this kind of comparison in UDA is very convenient; sometimes, only changing one line of code can complete this mission. As a result, a lot of time can be
saved by avoiding building a whole project which uses other libraries for comparison purposes.

Conclusion and Future Work

10.1

Conclusion

In this dissertation, I mainly discuss the usage of TensorCore in the numerical linear algebra field,
especially applying the recursive linear algebra algorithms on TensorCore. The first attempt is the
matrix-matrix operations on TensorCore (Chapter 4); it shows that with the recursive algorithms,
the matrix-matrix operations will be faster than the ordinary TensorCore-based GEMMs.
Further, I investigate the algorithms of the matrix factorization and find that most of the
conventional matrix factorization algorithms don’t have enough parallelism or data locality to be
executed quickly on TensorCore. To improve the performance, some recursive methods are devised
to convert the tall and skinny matrix-matrix multiplications to relatively square matrix-matrix
multiplications at the cost of more computations. The research product currently has two matrix factorization solutions, including QR factorization (Chapter 5) and eigenvalue decomposition
(Chapter 6). In terms of the QR factorization, I deployed the recursive Gram-Schmidt and Householder QR factorization on TensorCore, which brings over 10x speedup compared to cuSOLVER
SGEQRF routine. For eigenvalue decomposition, I replace the symmetric band reduction with the
faster TensorCore-based band reduction by using TSQR and WY representation instead of traditional ZY representation. I also did a case study on out-of-GPU-Core Gram-Schmidt QR factorization (Chapter 7), and the results indicate that the TensorCore can also help a lot with the recursive
strategy on out-of-GPU-Core linear algebra algorithms.
One of the challenges of using TensorCore is that the results are not very accurate because
it involves half-precision. In this dissertation, I also state some remedies to bring the accuracy
back to single precision. For instance, the TensorCore-based QR factorization can be used as a
preconditioner, and when solving the linear least square problems, we can use iterative methods
such as CGLS to recover the accuracy from singling even double precision. Another method is
using refined TCGEMMs that can directly bring the accuracy back to single precision in GEMMs.
The usage of TensorCore is not only limited to numerical linear algebra. Actually, the usage

152

of TensorCore in deep neural networks and some machine learning algorithms, especially kernel
machines, is more widely because these algorithms naturally have rich and big GEMMs. In Chapter
8, I discuss using TensorCore in support vector machine, and the performance beats the state-ofart ThunderSVM while keeping the same accuracy. In Chapter 9, I also investigate a framework
that supports the distributed TensorCore-based computations, which has high programmability,
flexibility, and productivity.

10.2

Future Work

Further research on TensorCore has many directions. The investigations on the numerical linear
algebra algorithms have been integrated and implemented in the LATER project14 , and it will be
very interesting to fill other linear algebra algorithms, including partial pivoting LU factorization
and Cholesky factorization in this project. In addition, as TensorCore generates accuracy loss, the
error analysis of the TensorCore-based applications will also be valuable. For example, figuring
out the reasons behind the good eigenvalues accuracy in the TensorCore-based band reduction may
guide the design pattern of new TensorCore-based algorithms. Besides, the kernel machine has been
overlooked for several years after the evolution of deep neural networks because of memory size and
computation speed limitations. But the latest GPU, such as A100, can perform mixed-precision
GEMMs with over 300 TFLOPs, which is 15x faster than the single precision GEMMs. The huge
speedup also encourages us to find out some potential methods related to kernel machines that can
be executed efficiently on or out of the GPU core.
